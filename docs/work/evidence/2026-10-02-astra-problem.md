# The problem I would try to solve

Written October 5, 2026, America/New_York; historical filename retained as requested. Raw-evidence baseline: ec01f4e8. This statement precedes external research and my plan. I have not opened the proposed method or design files. The permitted decision history includes summaries of other people's conclusions; I saw those incidentally and am not claiming perfect experimental blindness. I also led earlier harness work and therefore have prior commitments to challenge.

Ben wants useful software for his actual projects, with machines doing most execution and him making the decisions that depend on his purposes. Instead, work accumulates around a proposed solution. A failure becomes another task; completing that task counts as progress even when the original problem remains. Ben is asked to approve technical choices he cannot assess. By the time the accumulated design is visibly wrong, the investment and complexity make reconsideration expensive. The harness is exposed to exactly the same failure as the projects it was meant to help.

The problem is not simply unreliable agents or excessive code. It is that a locally successful development process can repeatedly select the wrong next investment. A plan can execute accurately while becoming less valuable. A test suite can verify an increasingly elaborate answer to an unverified question. A human approval can make a choice procedurally authorized without making it well informed.

The raw record supports concern without proving every explanation. The October 1 detritus report counts 216 untracked files, seven registered worktrees and 136 work records at its own observation time. Its component inventory names interacting census, work-record, messaging, cleanup, hook, publication and installation machinery. These are observations from reports, not independently reproduced historical snapshots. They do not by themselves establish that a component is unnecessary. Our directly recorded lane 62 sequence shows a seven-hour failure to resume, a missing reply link generating another alert, guard refusals, unauthorized retries and conflicting accounts of testing. More machinery did not prevent those failures. The history also contains successful reviewed merges; failure of the method must not be inferred by ignoring them.

The desired result is a way of working that can discover it is solving the wrong problem before much investment, change the architecture when a whole class of failures warrants it, and finish a useful increment whose quality and operating burden Ben can judge. Rethinking belongs at investment decisions, not after every tool call. Execution should retain enough memory to resume honestly without making the memory system another product.

What Ben may have incomplete: maximum automation, minimum coordination, minimum code and maximum quality can conflict. A redesign can destroy hard-won protections as easily as remove waste. A very simple implementation can push complexity into Ben's daily work. No method removes the need to decide which outcomes matter or to expose a design to actual use. Those are hypotheses to test, not reasons to abandon the aim. Likewise, expecting all four metrics to improve strictly is mathematically impossible when a baseline already has zero rework; preservation and improvement need distinct meanings.

Questions for Ben, not prerequisites for research: Which real project outcome would make the next two weeks worthwhile even if no harness improvement shipped? What failure would make that outcome unacceptable? How much reading and decision time are you willing to spend each week? Which existing safeguards protect losses you will not tolerate? When you say simpler, is the priority fewer concepts to understand, fewer manual steps, or fewer moving parts to operate? My starting assumption is that your first priority is a working project result with fewer concepts and less attention demanded from you.
